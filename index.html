Moydrum, Co. Westmeath

The year a field outside Athlone became the voice of a state

A century of Athlone music ran into a 60 kilowatt Marconi transmitter at Moydrum — and came out the other side as national radio, a national argument, and a national sound. Everything on this page turns on that one point.

One mast, three histories

Moydrum is not a footnote in Athlone's story. It is the hinge. The music came in from one side, the state's broadcasting service went out the other, and the argument about what Ireland should be allowed to hear broke out in the middle. Choose an arm to follow.

Out of the mast: the making of RTÉ

Irish national broadcasting did not begin at Moydrum, but it became national there. Before 1933 the signal barely cleared Dublin. After it, a farmhouse in Kerry and a parish hall in Donegal heard the same programme on the same evening.

1926

2RN opens in Dublin

A low-power station in Little Denmark Street. Listeners outside Leinster mostly hear it as a rumour, or not at all.

1932

The Congress relay

The unfinished Athlone plant is brought up early to carry the Eucharistic Congress to Europe and America. The proof of concept arrives before the official opening.

1933

Athlone goes to full power

De Valera opens the Moydrum station on 6 February. For the first time the whole island, and a good part of Britain and the continent, can hear Ireland talking to itself.

1938

Radio Éireann

The service takes the name it will carry for a generation, its main voice still leaving the country through Westmeath.

1966

Radio Telefís Éireann

Television is folded in and the organisation becomes RTÉ — the institution whose reach was first built on a midlands bog.

The fight over what came out of the speaker

Once one station could reach every kitchen in the country, its playlist stopped being a matter of taste. Within a year of Moydrum opening, the row over jazz had become a national campaign — and the man leading it had served his curacy in Athlone.

The sound

Harry Foy's quartette had been playing syncopated dance music in Athlone since 1919, a decade before the word "jazz" meant anything much to most of the country. Gramophones, dance halls and then the wireless carried it everywhere.

By the early 1930s the dance band was the default Saturday night across the midlands, and the new national station had its own house dance orchestra playing the same repertoire.

The campaign

On New Year's Day 1934 the Gaelic League marched in Mohill, Co. Leitrim, behind banners against jazz. The organiser was Fr Peter Conefrey, once a curate in Athlone, who took aim squarely at the broadcasters as well as the dance halls.

The argument was never only about music. It was about who chose what a whole country listened to after dark.

The Public Dance Halls Act followed in 1935, putting every dance in the state under licence from the District Court and handing local clergy and gardaí an effective veto.

What it settled

Very little, and a great deal. Dance halls were licensed, house dances withered, and the music kept playing — on the same national station the campaigners had denounced.

What the row really established was that Irish broadcasting was now a cultural instrument with a policy, and Athlone was the aerial it spoke through.

Twenty years separate Foy's first jazz band from the Act that tried to legislate it out of the parish hall. The thing that made the difference was a transmitter four miles east of the town.

Walk it

Five stops, from a plaque on a house in Irishtown to the mast footings at Moydrum and the mill walls at Clara. Select a stop to find it on the map.

Stop 1 · The Bawn, Irishtown Town centre

McCormack's birthplace

A bronze plaque marks the house where the tenor was born on 14 June 1884. Kilkelly found him singing in a choir a few streets from here.

Stop 2 · Chapel Street 0.4 km

Dean Crowe Theatre

Home of the Athlone Musical Society, founded by Henry Foy in 1902 and staging work here for over 120 years.

Stop 3 · St Mary's Place 0.6 km

Site of the Crescent Ballroom

The showband years in one building: the licensed dance hall the 1935 Act produced, packed every weekend through the 1950s and 60s.

Stop 4 · Moydrum 4.8 km east

The transmitting station

The surviving Marconi transmitter halls and mast footings — the pivot point of this whole story, and the proposed site of the museum.

Stop 5 · Clara, Co. Offaly 18 km via the greenway

The Goodbody mills

Where Marconi's 1898 spark-gap signal crossed between the flour mill and the jute works, thirty-five years before Moydrum.

What the museum would do

Four commitments, built around the site itself rather than around a collection in a cabinet.

I

Save what survives

Band instruments, recordings, paperwork and the remaining Moydrum valve gear, into one protected civic collection before any more of it goes.

II

Let people hear it

Working valve studio and listening rooms, so the story is told in sound rather than in captions on a wall.

III

Teach from it

Radio documentary and sound workshops with local schools, alongside traditional and jazz tuition in the town.

IV

Join it up

One route linking Athlone Castle, the Old Rail Trail Greenway, Moydrum and Clara's Marconi sites.

Add to the archive

Photographs, dance tickets, band programmes, tapes, a valve set in a shed, or a story you were told. Everything helps establish the case.
